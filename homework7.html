Homework 7 · Statistical Independence Analysis

Statistical Independence and Chi-Square Testing

Analyzing bivariate relationships through contingency tables, expected frequencies, chi-square statistics, and distinguishing statistical association from causality.

Overview

This homework explores the concept of statistical independence between two categorical variables. Given a bivariate dataset (X, Y), we will:

  • Programmatically construct contingency tables showing joint and marginal frequencies
  • Calculate conditional probabilities P(Y|X) and P(X|Y)
  • Compute expected frequencies under the assumption of independence
  • Calculate the chi-square (χ²) statistic and p-value to test independence
  • Interpret results in terms of statistical dependence vs. independence
  • Critically distinguish statistical association from causality

Theoretical Background

Statistical Independence

Definition (Probability): Two random variables X and Y are independent if and only if:

P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) · P(Y = y) for all x, y

Equivalently:

  • P(Y = y | X = x) = P(Y = y) (knowing X doesn't change Y's distribution)
  • P(X = x | Y = y) = P(X = x) (knowing Y doesn't change X's distribution)

Contingency Tables

A contingency table (cross-tabulation) displays the frequency distribution of two categorical variables:

X \ Y Y₁ Y₂ ... Yₘ Total
X₁ n₁₁ n₁₂ ... n₁ₘ n₁·
X₂ n₂₁ n₂₂ ... n₂ₘ n₂·
... ... ... ... ... ...
Xₖ nₖ₁ nₖ₂ ... nₖₘ nₖ·
Total n·₁ n·₂ ... n·ₘ n

Where:

  • nᵢⱼ = observed frequency (count) of (X=Xᵢ, Y=Yⱼ)
  • nᵢ· = row marginal (total for X=Xᵢ) = Σⱼ nᵢⱼ
  • n·ⱼ = column marginal (total for Y=Yⱼ) = Σᵢ nᵢⱼ
  • n = grand total = Σᵢ Σⱼ nᵢⱼ

Marginal and Conditional Frequencies

Marginal probabilities (estimated from data):

  • P̂(X = Xᵢ) = nᵢ· / n
  • P̂(Y = Yⱼ) = n·ⱼ / n

Conditional probabilities:

  • P̂(Y = Yⱼ | X = Xᵢ) = nᵢⱼ / nᵢ·
  • P̂(X = Xᵢ | Y = Yⱼ) = nᵢⱼ / n·ⱼ

Expected Frequencies Under Independence

If X and Y were independent, the expected frequency for cell (i, j) would be:

Eᵢⱼ = n · P(X = Xᵢ) · P(Y = Yⱼ) = (nᵢ· · n·ⱼ) / n

This is the frequency we would expect if the row and column variables were unrelated.

Chi-Square Test for Independence

The chi-square statistic measures the discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies:

χ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ [(Oᵢⱼ - Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ]

Where Oᵢⱼ = observed frequency, Eᵢⱼ = expected frequency under independence.

Degrees of freedom: df = (k - 1)(m - 1), where k = number of X categories, m = number of Y categories

Null hypothesis (H₀): X and Y are independent

Alternative hypothesis (H₁): X and Y are dependent (associated)

Decision rule:

  • If p-value < α (typically 0.05): Reject H₀, conclude variables are dependent
  • If p-value ≥ α: Fail to reject H₀, insufficient evidence of dependence

Association vs. Causality

Statistical association means the variables are not independent—knowing one provides information about the other.

Causality means one variable directly influences or causes changes in the other.

CRITICAL DISTINCTION: Association ≠ Causation

  • Correlation does not imply causation
  • Possible explanations for association:
    • X causes Y (direct causation)
    • Y causes X (reverse causation)
    • A confounding variable Z causes both X and Y
    • Coincidental correlation (spurious)
  • Establishing causality requires:
    • Controlled experiments (randomized controlled trials)
    • Temporal precedence (cause precedes effect)
    • Elimination of confounders
    • Plausible mechanism

Association vs. Causality: Critical Discussion

What the Chi-Square Test Tells Us

The chi-square test for independence answers one question: Are the two variables statistically associated?

  • If p < 0.05: We have evidence of association (dependence)
  • If p ≥ 0.05: No evidence of association (possibly independent)

What the Chi-Square Test Does NOT Tell Us

The test provides no information about causality. Association can arise from multiple mechanisms:

1. Direct Causation: X → Y

Example: Attack sophistication (X) → Breach success (Y)

More sophisticated attacks directly cause higher success rates.

2. Reverse Causation: Y → X

Example: Ice cream sales (X) correlate with crime rates (Y)

But high crime doesn't cause ice cream purchases—both are caused by warm weather.

3. Confounding Variable: Z → X and Z → Y

Example: Education level (Z) → Income (X) and Health outcomes (Y)

Income and health are associated, but education is the underlying cause of both.

4. Spurious Correlation

Example: Number of Nicolas Cage films (X) correlates with swimming pool drownings (Y)

Pure coincidence with no causal mechanism.

Establishing Causality

To move from association to causation, we need:

  • Randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Randomly assign treatment to eliminate confounding
  • Temporal precedence: Cause must precede effect in time
  • Dose-response relationship: Stronger cause → stronger effect
  • Consistency: Relationship holds across different studies and populations
  • Plausibility: Biologically/mechanistically plausible pathway
  • Specificity: Specific cause → specific effect (not always required)

In Cybersecurity Context

Observed association: Systems with outdated software are more frequently breached.

Possible explanations:

  1. Causal: Outdated software has vulnerabilities → higher breach probability
  2. Confounding: Organizations with poor security culture both fail to update software AND have weak overall defenses
  3. Reverse: Breached organizations delay updates during incident response

How to determine causality:

  • Controlled experiment: randomly assign software update status to similar systems
  • Longitudinal study: track systems over time, observe breach risk before and after updates
  • Control for confounders: adjust for organization size, industry, security budget, etc.

Statistical vs. Practical Significance

Statistical significance (p < 0.05): Unlikely to occur by chance alone

Practical significance: Effect size is large enough to matter in real-world decisions

  • With large n, even tiny effects become statistically significant but may be practically irrelevant
  • With small n, large effects may not reach statistical significance but could be important
  • Always consider effect size (e.g., Cramér's V, odds ratios) alongside p-values

Conclusions

Key Takeaways

  • Contingency tables organize bivariate categorical data, showing joint, marginal, and conditional frequencies
  • Independence means P(X,Y) = P(X)·P(Y) — knowing one variable provides no information about the other
  • Expected frequencies under independence are (row total × column total) / n
  • Chi-square statistic quantifies discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies
  • P-value indicates whether observed association is unlikely under the null hypothesis of independence
  • Association ≠ Causation: Statistical tests detect relationships but cannot establish causal direction
  • Causality requires: Controlled experiments, temporal precedence, and elimination of confounders

Practical Applications in Cybersecurity

  • Anomaly detection: Identifying unusual associations between user behavior and access patterns
  • Threat intelligence: Correlating attack vectors with attacker profiles
  • Risk assessment: Quantifying relationships between vulnerabilities and exploitation
  • Incident analysis: Understanding which factors are associated with successful attacks

Limitations and Cautions

  • Chi-square test assumes sufficient sample size (expected frequencies ≥ 5 in each cell)
  • For small samples, use Fisher's exact test instead
  • P-values are sensitive to sample size—report effect sizes too
  • Multiple testing increases false positive rate—use corrections (e.g., Bonferroni)
  • Always critically evaluate whether observed associations reflect real phenomena or artifacts
